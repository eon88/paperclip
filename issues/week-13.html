Issue 13 of 52  ·  Phase 2 — The Broken Transmission

The Feminization of Everything — What Got Lost in the Balance

This topic requires precision, because it's easy to say it wrong in both directions.

One way to say it wrong: to argue that masculinity is under siege, that women are to blame, that society has conspired against men, and that the solution is to push back against anything associated with femininity. That's grievance dressed up as analysis. It isn't useful, and it isn't accurate.

The other way to say it wrong: to pretend that an institutional and cultural shift toward exclusively feminine values hasn't happened in a number of the environments where young men spend most of their time — particularly schools — or that this shift carries no cost for boys and young men. That's denial dressed up as open-mindedness.

The honest position is somewhere between those, and it requires looking at what specifically changed.

What schools were designed to do — and what they became

For much of the twentieth century, boys and girls were educated in mixed environments with a variety of pedagogical approaches. The shift toward what's often called the "modern classroom" — more sedentary, more verbal, more focused on collaborative learning and emotional expression — began in earnest in the 1990s and accelerated from there.

That shift had real benefits for many learners. It also had a cost that was largely unexamined.

Boys, on average, learn differently than girls on average. They tend to do better with movement, competition, challenge, direct feedback, and clear hierarchy. They respond poorly to extended periods of sedentary seat work, vague encouragement, and the suppression of physical energy. These are not universal — individuals vary — but they're statistically significant enough to explain part of why boys' outcomes in education began to diverge from girls' in the same period that the classroom environment shifted.

The issue isn't that girls were accommodated. The issue is that boys were simultaneously un-accommodated without anyone clearly naming that that was happening.

The disappearance of masculine virtues from institutional culture

Beyond pedagogy, something subtler happened. A set of qualities that were once openly valued and cultivated in young men — toughness, directness, competition, the willingness to absorb hardship without complaint, physical and moral courage — gradually moved from celebrated to suspect in many institutional settings.

Not because those qualities are bad. They aren't. But because, in certain forms, they can be misapplied — and the institutional response to the misapplication was to pathologize the underlying quality rather than to teach its right use.

A boy who is aggressive is a problem to be managed, rather than a boy with energy that needs to be channeled into something constructive. A boy who is competitive needs to be redirected toward collaboration, rather than given a worthy competition to win. A boy who takes risks is a liability, rather than a boy whose risk-tolerance could become genuine courage if it's shaped appropriately.

The result: a generation of boys who grew up in environments that treated their nature as an inconvenience, and who received very little instruction in how to turn that nature into something valuable.

What was lost in the process

What was lost is harder to describe than the gains were — which is partly why nobody noticed. The gains were visible: more girls in college, more women in professions, more explicit attention to emotional intelligence in the culture. All real, all good.

The losses were invisible, because they were about formation that didn't happen. Boys who weren't taught to compete well — with honor, with dignity, without cruelty. Boys who weren't shown that enduring discomfort builds something inside them. Boys who weren't given the explicit message that their strength is a responsibility, not a threat. Boys who were taught to apologize for their nature rather than to develop it.

The actual problem isn't gender — it's incomplete formation

Here's the honest summary: what got lost in the cultural shift wasn't masculinity as such. What got lost was mature masculinity — the cultivation of the specific virtues that turn a boy's raw energy and drive into something that can actually serve the people around him.

Raw masculinity without cultivation is destructive. Cultivated masculinity is exactly what the world needs more of. The problem is that the cultivation step got dropped out of the process — in homes, in schools, in institutions — not because of malice, but because the system forgot how to do it, or stopped believing it was worth doing.

Your job isn't to resent what was lost. Your job is to go find the cultivation that was skipped.

That process — deliberately cultivating the virtues that your raw nature could become — is not just personal development. It's a contribution to something that's been missing for a generation. It matters beyond you.
