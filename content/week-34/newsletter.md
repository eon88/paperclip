# Life Compass — Issue 34

**Subject:** The lies you tell yourself are the expensive ones.

---

You're probably not a liar. You don't tell obvious falsehoods to other people with deliberate intent.

But you almost certainly lie to yourself. And that's the more costly kind.

The lies are ordinary. I'll start Monday. That relationship is fine. I'm working on it. I don't really drink that much. I just need to get this one thing handled first. These sound reasonable. They are very effective at keeping the real picture blurry. And the blurry picture is exactly the problem — you can't move from where you are if you've convinced yourself you're somewhere else.

Self-deception isn't weakness. It's the mind managing the gap between who you are and who you think you should be. When that gap is uncomfortable, the mind softens it — finds reasons, extenuating circumstances, explanations. It constructs a story in which you are not failing, just waiting for better conditions.

The problem: it prevents change. You cannot address a problem you've explained away.

Here's the first test. Look at your actions, not your intentions. Your values are not what you say they are. They're what you do consistently. If you say health matters and you haven't exercised in months — you don't actually prioritize health right now. If you say relationships matter and you're barely present for the people who count — that's the revealed truth, whatever the stated one is.

Go through what you say matters most. Ask honestly: does how I spend my time reflect it? This is uncomfortable for almost every man who actually does it. That discomfort is the start of something.

Second test: what are you not saying out loud? The things you're lying to yourself about are usually the things you won't speak aloud to anyone — because saying them makes them real, and real means you have to deal with them. You know what they are. The relationship on the wrong trajectory. The job that's been wrong for two years. The thing you've been "almost ready" to do for longer than you can honestly say.

Say it out loud. To yourself, to paper, to someone you trust. The moment it's spoken, you can no longer pretend you don't see it.

Third test: when you explain your life, who are you convincing? If the explanation is very polished — covers every angle, has a reason for every failure, is very hard to fault — that's a sign. Real self-assessment is rougher. A man who actually sees himself clearly doesn't need a perfect explanation.

This week, write down honest answers to three questions:
- What did I say I would do this week that I didn't do?
- What am I currently tolerating that I know I shouldn't?
- What do I know is true about my situation that I've been avoiding saying clearly?

Don't keep these in your head. Write them. The mind softens things it holds internally. Paper doesn't.

The man who sees himself accurately can change. He may not like everything he sees. But he's working with the truth, and the truth is the only thing you can build anything real on.

**This week's anchor:** Answer those three questions honestly, in writing, before the week ends.

— Life Compass
