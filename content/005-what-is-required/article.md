# Stop Asking What You Want. Start Asking What's Required.

> **Pillar:** Responsibility | **Idea:** 005 | **Status:** Ready to publish

---

I want to give you a question that will change how you make decisions.

Not "what do I want?" But "what is required?"

That swap sounds small. It isn't. Most men are running their entire lives off the wrong question — and the results show it.

---

## The Wanting Game

Ask a young man what he wants and he'll tell you fast. Money. Freedom. A girl who's worth it. Respect. To not have to answer to anyone. To wake up when he wants, do what he wants, build something of his own. The wanting is real. There's nothing wrong with wanting those things.

The problem is using "what do I want?" as your compass.

That question is self-referential. It loops back to you every time. The answer it generates is always about your preferences, your comfort, your feelings in this moment. And because feelings shift — sometimes hourly — so does the direction. You want to start the business until it gets hard. You want to get in shape until it means turning down the Friday night. You want the relationship until she asks for consistency you don't know how to give.

The wanting never stops. The doing barely starts.

This is hedonistic drift. It doesn't announce itself. It just quietly stacks — month after month, year after year — until you look up and realize you've been very busy chasing things that haven't actually built anything. You've been optimizing for what feels good in the present, and the future version of you has been paying the bill.

---

## The Question That Changes the Frame

"What does this moment require of me?"

That's a different kind of question. It doesn't ask about your preferences. It asks about your responsibility. It pulls the focus off you and onto the situation — the relationship, the commitment, the craft, the person in front of you, the hour you're in.

It's the question a soldier asks when he'd rather sleep. The question a father asks at 3am when the baby won't stop crying. The question a craftsman asks when he's tired but the work still has another hour left in it. Not "what do I feel like doing?" but "what does this situation call for?"

The answer to that question is almost always clear. You already know. You've known for a while. The wanting question lets you pretend you don't.

---

## Why Entitlement Feeds on "What Do I Want?"

Entitlement is what happens when your wants become demands. When you've spent so long treating your preferences as the organizing principle of your life that you start to believe the world — your job, your relationship, your city, your body — owes you an arrangement that matches those preferences.

And "what do I want?" is the engine of entitlement. You ask it, you get an answer, and then every obstacle between you and that answer starts to feel like an injustice. The job that asks too much. The woman who needs more than you're offering. The body that doesn't respond as fast as you think it should. The business that takes longer to build than you expected.

None of these are injustices. They're the reality of actually trying to build something. But if your compass is want-based, they'll feel wrong. And that feeling will quietly convince you that you deserve better circumstances before you can give your best effort.

The required question breaks this. "What is required of me here?" doesn't care about your preferences. It doesn't ask whether the situation is fair. It just asks what you owe the moment.

---

## What "Required" Actually Looks Like in Practice

This isn't a philosophy. It's a daily operating system.

When you sit down to work and your focus is scattered — not "what do I feel like doing?" but "what does this hour require?" When you're in a conversation with someone you care about and you're distracted — not "do I feel like being present?" but "what does this person require from me right now?" When you're tired and the workout is still on the calendar — not "do I feel like going?" but "what does the version of me I'm trying to build require?"

You stop outsourcing the decision to your mood. The mood is irrelevant. The requirement is what's real.

Marcus Aurelius wrote to himself — not for an audience, not for posterity — "What am I doing with my soul? Ask yourself this question every hour." That's the same frame. Not "am I enjoying this?" Not "is this what I pictured?" Just: what is called for here, and am I giving it?

The men you most respect operate this way. Not because they've killed desire — they still want things. But their wanting doesn't run the show. Obligation runs the show. Responsibility to something outside themselves runs the show. They've learned to lead with the required question and let the wanting follow.

---

## The Commitment Problem

Young men today have a follow-through problem. Not an ability problem — a commitment problem. They start things. They don't finish them. They enroll in the program, hit the gym for three weeks, launch the project at full energy, then drift when the initial rush fades.

The wanting question is why. You started because you wanted to. The wanting faded. So did the effort.

When you ask what's required instead, you're building a different kind of agreement with yourself. Not "I'll do this as long as it feels good." But "I said this mattered. What does it require from me today?" That's a contract. Contracts don't expire when your mood shifts.

This is the difference between a man who trains and a man who used to train. A man who builds and a man who had a good idea once. The required question creates durability. The wanting question creates a highlight reel with a lot of gaps in it.

Commit based on what's required, and you stop needing motivation as a precondition for work. The requirement is enough. It's always been enough. You just kept letting the wanting question talk you out of it.

---

## The Hidden Gift in This Shift

Here's what nobody tells you: when you stop making "what do I want?" your primary question, you don't get less. You get more. You get it later, and you get it deeper.

The man who asks what's required of him — in his work, in his relationships, in his character — becomes someone people trust. He shows up when it's inconvenient. He delivers when it costs him something. He doesn't vanish when the return isn't obvious. That man attracts mentors. That man gets handed opportunities. That man earns real respect — not the kind that comes from performance, but the kind that comes from proven reliability.

And quietly, underneath all of it, he earns his own respect. Which is the thing most young men are actually chasing when they say they want money or freedom or a woman worth having. They want to think well of themselves. They want to feel like a man whose word means something.

You don't get that by asking what you want. You get it by asking what's required — and then doing it.

---

**Your challenge:** Before Friday, pick one area of your life where you've been defaulting to "what do I want?" — and swap the question. Ask what that relationship, that commitment, or that goal actually requires from you this week. Write it down. Then do it before you ask yourself whether you feel like it.

That's the whole assignment.

The wanting will still be there. But now it follows something real.
