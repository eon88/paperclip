# Life Compass — Issue 12

**Subject:** Content about manhood isn't forming you

---

The switch didn't happen with a decision. Nobody said: from now on, young men will get their formation from screens instead of from older men. It just happened — because the screens were available and the older men were increasingly not.

By the time anyone noticed, an entire generation had been raised on mediated images of manhood rather than the real thing.

Let's be specific about what a mentor actually was — because the word has been so corporatized it's lost its weight.

A mentor was an older man in your actual life who knew you specifically, cared whether you turned out well, and had enough experience to push you in directions you couldn't find on your own. He gave honest feedback without managing your feelings. He set a standard beyond what you currently were. He believed in your capacity before you had evidence to believe in it yourself. He did all of this over time, in the context of real shared work — not in formal appointments.

That relationship produced something that couldn't be produced any other way.

When it went missing, screens filled the space. Not intentionally — they were just there, and good at producing something resembling what young men were hungry for. Athletes, creators, influencers — confident, skilled, bold, endlessly accessible.

But the mediation is the problem. You can watch a creator for a thousand hours and still not know how he handles a genuine setback, because the camera is only on when he wants it to be. You see the persona. You never see the man.

Here's what the screen cannot do: a mentor could tell you something uncomfortable about yourself inside a relationship that also communicated belief in you. That combination is what made hard feedback land and stick. A screen delivers to an audience, not to you specifically. The feedback loops are popularity-optimized, not truth-optimized.

And the algorithm makes it worse. It learns what you engage with and feeds you more. The mediated manhood you receive gets shaped entirely by your existing preferences — which means it tends to confirm what you already believe rather than challenge it. A good mentor was most valuable when he pointed out what you couldn't see. The algorithm removes that. It builds a hall of mirrors while you imagine you're being formed.

Use content — but use it in proportion. Ask of everything you consume: is this helping me become something, or is it substituting for what would actually help?

Content should be a supplement to real relationships with real men who can see your actual blind spots. Not a replacement.

**This week's anchor:** Calculate roughly how many hours in the last month you spent consuming content about men — and how many hours you spent in actual conversation with a man you respect. Adjust the ratio.

— Life Compass
