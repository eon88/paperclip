**2/7**
A man without a personal code gets shaped by whatever is loudest:
Culture. Peer pressure. Convenience. Money. The path of least resistance.
He doesn't make decisions — he reacts.
Every compromise feels temporary and none of them are.
That's the life without a code.

**3/7**
A code is not a list of vague values.
"I value integrity" is not a code.
A code is specific:
"I tell the truth even when it costs me."
"I don't take money for work I don't respect."
"I don't stay in relationships where I'm not being honest."
Specific enough to know immediately when you've violated it.

**4/7**
The power of a code is pre-commitment.
You decide what you will and won't do BEFORE the temptation arrives.
This is not rigidity — it's freedom.
The man who has already decided doesn't agonize at the decision point.
He already knows. He acts on what he knows.

**5/7**
The code becomes real when you hold it under pressure.
Not when it's easy — when it costs something.
When the opportunity requires you to compromise one of your lines.
When the relationship pressures you to look the other way.
When the money is on the table and your code says no.
That's when the code matters. Build it before you need it.

**6/7**
A code needs three things to be real:
Specific — you know exactly when you've violated it.
Tested — you've held it when it cost you something.
Yours — genuinely arrived at, not inherited or performed.
Anything less is decoration.

**7/7**
Write the first draft of your code this week.
Three to five statements. Specific enough to test.
Not aspirational — honest.
What do you actually hold yourself to right now?
What do you want to hold yourself to?
That draft is the beginning.

## LinkedIn Post

The leaders who are most trusted share a specific characteristic: they are consistent. Not predictably boring — predictably principled. You know what they will and won't do before you need to test it.

That predictability isn't personality. It's a code — a set of specific, pre-committed positions on what they stand for and what they won't compromise. And because the positions were established before pressure arrives, the behavior under pressure is consistent.

The alternative — improvising ethical and strategic positions in the moment of temptation — produces inconsistency that people around you eventually notice and eventually stop trusting.

This matters in organizational contexts because culture flows downward. The code a leader actually holds (not the values on the wall) is what defines the culture of the team. And teams whose leaders are improvising their principles produce teams that improvise theirs.

The practical exercise: write three to five specific commitments — specific enough that you'd know immediately if you violated one. Test them. Hold them when they cost something. That's what makes them real.

A man with a code is a man who can be trusted. That's worth building.

## Short-Form Clip Recommendations

### Clip 1 — "What will you not do, regardless of the incentive?"
**Moment:** The opening question as a direct address to the viewer. Followed by the explanation of why this is the test of whether you have a code.
**Why it works:** Opens with a question that most viewers can't immediately answer, which creates the productive discomfort that drives engagement. The question itself is the content — it doesn't need much setup. Best at 20–25 seconds, delivered directly, let the question sit.
**Hook line:** "I want you to answer a question honestly. Most men can't."

### Clip 2 — "Pre-commitment is freedom"
**Moment:** The reframe that having a code is not rigidity — it's freedom. The man who has already decided doesn't agonize. He already knows.
**Why it works:** Addresses the most common objection to codes and principles — that they constrain. The counter-argument (they liberate by removing agonizing) is genuinely surprising and true. Best at 40 seconds, building from the objection to the resolution, ending on "that's what a code gives you."
**Hook line:** "Most men think having a code constrains them. The opposite is true."
