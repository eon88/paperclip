# The Man You're Aiming At — How to Build a Clear Picture of Who You're Becoming

**Week:** 27 of 52
**Phase:** Phase 4 — Building Your Own Template

---

Most men have a vague sense of wanting to be better. They want to be more disciplined, more focused, more successful, more respected. They can feel the direction — toward something more solid, more purposeful, more worthy of the life they're living.

What most men don't have is a clear picture.

Not a vision board. Not a list of goals. A genuine, detailed, lived-in picture of the specific man they are trying to become — what he believes, how he moves through the world, how he treats people, what his days look like, what he has built, what he has refused.

This matters because direction without destination is drift wearing a better costume.

## Why Most Men Stay Vague

The resistance to building a clear picture of the target self is almost always fear, though it rarely presents that way. It presents as practicality: "I don't want to box myself in." It presents as humility: "I don't know what I'll want in ten years." It presents as flexibility: "I want to stay open to different possibilities."

These are reasonable things to say. They are also, frequently, a way of avoiding the commitment that a clear picture requires. Because once you have built the picture — once you have said, specifically, this is who I am trying to become — you are also implicitly saying that your current self falls short of it. And that gap, once seen clearly, is demanding. It doesn't let you off the hook.

The vagueness is more comfortable. But the man who stays vague about who he's trying to become will mostly stay who he is.

## What the Picture Actually Looks Like

The man you're aiming at is not a fantasy. He is a projection — the version of you that has done the work, made the hard decisions, kept the commitments, and built what you are capable of building.

He is not richer, necessarily. Not more famous. Not free of difficulty or struggle. He is more solid. More directed. More at home in himself. He knows what he stands for and behaves accordingly. He has built something worth having built. He shows up for the people who need him. He carries weight without looking for credit.

Building the picture requires specificity. Not "I want to be disciplined" — what does a disciplined version of you do differently at 6am than you do now? Not "I want to have strong relationships" — specifically who are the people in your life, and what kind of presence are you in their lives? Not "I want to do meaningful work" — what is the work, what has it produced, and who has it served?

The picture should be detailed enough that you could recognize the man if you met him. It should be specific enough to make decisions by. When you're unsure what to do in a given situation, you should be able to ask: what would that man do? And get an answer.

## Building It Practically

A useful exercise: write a letter from the version of yourself ten years from now, back to you today. Not the letter you wish you could receive — the honest one. The one that describes, in specific detail, what the next ten years built. What was hard. What was worth it. What you had to give up and what you got in return. What your life looks like and why you don't regret the choices that produced it.

This exercise does something that abstract goal-setting doesn't: it puts you into the perspective of the man who has already done the work and looks back with knowledge of how it went. That perspective tends to produce clarity. It tends to reveal what actually matters from what merely seemed to matter. It tends to strip away the goals that are really about status or approval and surface the ones that are really about becoming someone you can live with.

When you have a draft of the picture, ask: is this actually what I want, or is it what I think I should want? The man you're aiming at has to be someone you genuinely care about becoming — not a performance of success, not the version of you your parents would approve of, not the version of you that would impress people at a reunion. The man you'll actually do the hard work to become has to matter to you in a way that survives a bad week.

## The Picture as a Tool

Once you have the picture, it becomes a decision-making tool.

Every significant choice can be measured against it. Does this move me toward or away from who I'm trying to become? Does this relationship, this job, this habit, this use of Saturday morning — does it build the man in the picture or erode him?

This is not perfectionism. You will not always choose the thing that builds. The picture is not a judge. It is a compass. It doesn't tell you that you failed — it tells you which direction to return to.

The man in the picture is not finished either. The picture will update as you do. But the clarity of having it — of knowing who you are actually aiming at — is one of the most useful things you can give yourself.

Build the picture. Revise it as you grow. Let it govern the choices that compound into who you become.
