# Life Compass — Issue 25

**Subject:** You can't ask for a mentor. You have to earn one.

---

Most young men who want mentorship go about it in a way almost perfectly designed to ensure they never get it.

They wait for a mentor to appear. They look for someone to adopt them. They want a man further along who will choose them, invest in them without being asked, and guide them generously. This is a reasonable desire. It is also a strategy that produces nothing.

Here is the honest picture.

A living mentor is not a life coach. He is a man further along the road you're trying to travel who is willing to share what he's learned — whose example you can observe over time. The best mentoring relationships don't begin with a formal request. They begin with a man showing up somewhere serious work is being done, doing that work well, and gradually earning the attention of someone further along.

The man who becomes your mentor is usually not someone you identified as a mentor. He is someone who noticed you doing something right and kept watching.

Here's the uncomfortable part: most young men are not bringing anything worth mentoring. Mentors are not scarce because experienced men are selfish. They are scarce because young men have rarely prepared themselves to deserve the relationship.

The preparation looks like this: a specific and genuine competence. A reputation — even a small one — for reliability. Specific questions rather than vague needs. Evidence that advice given would actually be acted on.

A man who has done this work is not difficult to mentor. He is a pleasure. Experienced men want to invest in someone like this because the investment produces something.

You find living mentors in the places where work that matters to you is being done — not by networking in a transactional sense, but by being present, contributing, and becoming visible as a man doing real work.

When you've identified someone worth learning from: do not ask to "pick their brain." Do not say you're looking for a mentor. Lead with a specific observation about their work that shows you've actually studied it. Ask one specific question. Offer something concrete that might be useful to them.

If you get a response, honor it fully. Do what you said you'd do. Come back with evidence that you heard and acted on what was shared. Nothing builds a mentoring relationship faster than demonstrating that you're the kind of man who actually implements.

The relationship that forms from that foundation doesn't feel like formal mentorship. It feels like a man further along the road taking genuine interest in how you're traveling it — occasionally telling you what he sees in you that you can't see in yourself.

That is irreplaceable. You cannot read your way to it. You have to earn it.

**This week's anchor:** Identify one person further along the road you're trying to travel. Before you consider reaching out to them, ask honestly: what would you offer them? What evidence do you have that you're worth investing in? If the answer is thin, start there first.

— Life Compass
