# Why Your Generation Was Never Taught What You Were Made For

**Week:** 14 of 52
**Phase:** Phase 2 — The Broken Transmission

---

There's something strange about modern life that most men feel but few can name: the sense that they arrived at adulthood missing something — not a skill exactly, but a frame. A way of understanding what their life is for and what they're supposed to be doing with it.

Previous generations didn't talk about this much. Not because they were more enlightened, but because they mostly didn't have to. The frame was handed to them — not formally, not through lectures or curricula, but through proximity. Through watching the men around them live with purpose and carry weight. Through being given real responsibility early. Through a culture that had a relatively clear, if imperfect, picture of what a man was supposed to become.

That picture was not handed to your generation. The question is why.

## The Conversation That Stopped

For most of human history, the formation of young men was considered a serious cultural task. Societies built structures around it — rites of passage, apprenticeships, military service, religious formation, guild systems. Some of these structures were flawed. Some were brutal. But they shared a common understanding: a boy does not become a man automatically. Manhood is a set of capacities — discipline, courage, responsibility, the ability to carry others — that have to be developed. And that development doesn't happen by accident.

At some point in the late twentieth century, the conversation about masculine formation largely stopped. Not because it was solved. Because it became politically complicated to have.

The legitimate critique of toxic masculinity — of the ways male dominance had caused real harm — merged, in many cultural spaces, with a broader discomfort with the project of specifically forming men at all. If the old model of masculinity was problematic, and if any new model risked being called oppressive, the safer institutional response was to simply not have the conversation. To treat boys like smaller adults who needed no specific formation. To assume that the qualities worth developing would emerge on their own.

They didn't.

## What Got Removed Without a Replacement

The old structures that had formed men — even imperfectly — were dismantled without replacement. Rites of passage were abandoned as primitive. Apprenticeships disappeared as economies modernized. Religious formation declined as institutions lost authority. Military service ceased to be a near-universal experience. The extended male communities — workshops, trades, fraternal organizations — gradually dissolved or became irrelevant.

What replaced them? Mostly nothing. The assumption was that a boy raised in a good home with access to education would develop the inner resources he needed through some organic process. But the organic process had always depended on proximity — on watching and working alongside men who had already developed those resources. Without the proximity, the process didn't happen.

The result is a generation of men who are highly educated in many respects, capable in many technical domains, but who received almost no explicit formation in the capacities that historically defined mature manhood: the management of fear, the development of will, the discipline to carry weight consistently over time, the orientation toward contribution over consumption.

## The Schools Didn't Fill the Gap

It would be fair to ask whether formal education picked up the slack. It didn't — and largely couldn't.

Schools are designed to transmit knowledge and develop certain cognitive capacities. They are not designed to form character, develop courage, or teach a young man what he's for. The teachers who do manage to do some of this are doing it as individuals, through the force of their own character, not because the system is built to support it.

The curriculum does not include: how to face difficulty without running from it. How to build discipline when you don't feel motivated. What it means to give your word and keep it. How to carry responsibility for other people without being crushed by it. How to build something over years when the results are invisible in the short term.

These are not supplementary skills. They are the foundation that every other capacity is built on. And your generation, by and large, had to figure them out alone — or not at all.

## It Wasn't Malice. It Was Forgetting.

The important thing to understand about this is that no one did it to you on purpose.

The institutions that stopped forming men didn't decide to abandon young men. They were responding to real problems, making decisions that seemed reasonable at the time, and failing to notice what was being lost in the process. The father who was too busy or too absent wasn't choosing to deprive his son. He was living inside a world that had stopped telling him his role as a transmitter of formation was important.

Understanding this matters because it changes the emotional register of what you're dealing with. You are not the victim of a conspiracy. You are the inheritor of a series of disconnections, each one individually understandable, whose cumulative effect was that the formation you needed wasn't there when you needed it.

## You Can Get It Now

The formation that was supposed to happen earlier can still happen. The capacities that were supposed to be developed through those structures can still be developed — deliberately, intentionally, later than they should have been.

That's what the rest of this year is about. Not catching up on something you missed. Building something real — the frame your generation was never handed — with your own hands, from first principles, starting now.

You're not behind. You're beginning. Those are different things.
