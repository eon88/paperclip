Finding Your Examples — How to Build a Shelf of Men Worth Studying

You cannot aim at a target you cannot see. This is obvious in archery and almost entirely ignored in character development.

Men who are trying to become something better — more disciplined, more honorable, more capable — almost always work with abstract goals. They want to be better. They want to be the kind of man who handles things. They want to be someone their family can count on. These are genuine desires. But they are not targets. They are directions without a destination.

To build something, you need a picture of what you're building. Not a perfect, fully-specified blueprint — but a real image. Something concrete enough that you can look at a decision in front of you and ask: would the man I'm trying to become do this?

That image does not come from aspirational language. It comes from examples.

Why Examples Work

The human brain does not learn values from instruction as effectively as it learns them from observation. This is not a flaw in your design — it is your design. For most of human history, this was the mechanism of transmission. Boys learned how to be men by watching men. The knowledge was carried in behavior, not in text.

When you find a real man — living or dead, personally known or historically documented — who embodies something you want to develop, something happens differently than when you read a principle. You can see what the principle looks like in action. You can observe how it handles edge cases. You can ask, when facing a decision, not "what should a disciplined man do?" but "what would this specific man do?"

The specific is more powerful than the abstract. It is more memorable. It is more actionable. It is more honest — because a real man with real flaws who embodied something well is more instructive than a perfect ideal that no one has ever actually achieved.

How to Build the Shelf

The shelf is a mental collection of men worth studying — men whose character or competence or particular quality gives you something concrete to aim at.

You build it deliberately. You do not wait for it to assemble itself. Here is how:

Start with the quality you most want to develop right now. Not the full list. One quality. Pick the one that, if you developed it this year, would most change the trajectory of your life.

Now ask: who embodies this? Not who talks about it — who actually lives it? Look in your immediate environment first. Is there a man in your life — a relative, a colleague, an older man from your community — who has this quality in a form you can observe? If yes, he goes on the shelf.

Then look further. History, biography, literature. Who are the men across history who are known specifically for this quality? Theodore Roosevelt for physical courage and political tenacity. Frederick Douglass for moral courage under conditions that should have broken him. C.S. Lewis for intellectual honesty. Marcus Aurelius for the gap between power and restraint. Abraham Lincoln for steadiness under catastrophic pressure.

You are not hero-worshipping. You are building a reference library. These men had flaws. Some had significant ones. The flaws don't disqualify them as examples in the specific quality you're studying, any more than a man who is a poor husband should be dismissed as an example of brilliant engineering.

What to Do With the Shelf

The shelf is not decorative. It is operational.

When you face a decision that implicates the quality you're developing, you consult it. When you're tempted to take the easy route in a moment that requires honesty, you call to mind the man on your shelf who chose differently in harder circumstances. This is not performance. It is the same process by which apprentices have always learned from masters — by holding the master's standard present while they work.

Read the biographies of the men on your shelf. Not the summaries — the actual accounts of their lives. See how they made specific decisions under specific pressures. See where they failed and how they responded to failure. The texture of a real life is more instructive than any distilled wisdom extracted from it.

Over time, the shelf grows. It begins to represent a coherent picture of manhood — assembled not from a single idealized figure, but from the best qualities of several real men. Your father's steadiness. Your grandfather's work ethic. Frederick Douglass's moral courage. Your old coach's accountability to his word.

The Anti-Hero Problem

A word about who belongs on the shelf and who does not.

Modern culture tends to admire men who are interesting for destructive reasons. The charismatic criminal. The self-destructive genius. The man who didn't care about anyone's rules. There is something compelling about this archetype — the freedom it seems to embody, the defiance.

But you are not building a shelf of interesting men. You are building a shelf of men whose lives you want to aim at. The relevant question is not: was this man compelling? It is: was this man's life a good life? Did the qualities he embodied produce something worth having?

Choose accordingly.
