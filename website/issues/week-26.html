Writing Your Own Code — The Values That Will Govern Your Life

Most men operate on borrowed values — principles absorbed from their environment without ever being consciously examined or chosen. They work hard because their father worked hard. They distrust authority because something happened in high school. They pursue money because that's what success looked like in the culture they grew up in. They avoid commitment because they watched commitment fail.

None of this is wrong, exactly. Values absorbed from experience and environment are real. But they are not your code. They are a first draft written by other people and other circumstances. And a man who has never examined that draft — who has never asked which of these principles he would actually choose, which he would discard, and which are missing entirely — is a man who can be moved off his foundation by pressure, temptation, or a compelling argument at the wrong moment.

The work of writing your own code is the work of becoming ungovernable by accident.

What a Code Actually Is

A personal code is not a mission statement. It is not a list of aspirations. It is a set of commitments — principles you have decided you will hold regardless of circumstances, not because they're convenient but because you have decided they define who you are.

The difference matters. Aspirations are who you want to be. A code is who you've decided to be. Aspiration is soft; it bends under pressure. Code is load-bearing. It's what you come back to when things get hard, when the easier path would be to compromise, when the people around you are doing something different and making it look reasonable.

A code is also not purely personal. It exists in relation to other people — it governs how you treat them, what you owe them, what you will and will not do regardless of what they're doing to you. The man without a code is a man whose behavior is determined entirely by circumstances and incentives. The man with a code has something that survives circumstances.

Where Real Values Come From

Real values — the kind that hold under pressure — don't come from reflection alone. They come from the intersection of reflection and experience.

You discover what you actually value by paying attention to what you can't live with. The moments of deepest discomfort — when you compromised something and felt it afterward, when someone treated you in a way that felt fundamentally wrong, when you let someone down and couldn't shake the weight of it — these are data. They are pointing at something you care about more than you knew.

You discover what you value by looking at the men you most respect and asking what specifically you respect. Not "he's successful" or "he's a good person" — what specifically? His willingness to tell the truth when it costs him something? The way he shows up for his family without fanfare? His refusal to complain? The specific qualities that make you feel something when you witness them are already your values, waiting to be named.

You discover what you value by testing principles under pressure. You don't know if honesty is truly a value until you've told the truth when lying would have been easier and better for you in the short term. The value that holds when it's costly is the real one.

Writing It Down

The act of writing your code is not bureaucracy. It is the transformation of implicit commitments into explicit ones — and that transformation changes how they function.

An implicit value can be negotiated with in the moment. "I value honesty" is easier to set aside under pressure than "I do not lie, including to myself, including when the truth costs me something." The specificity is the point. The more precisely you can state the principle, the harder it is to rationalize your way around it.

Write the code as a set of commitments, not qualities. Not "I am honest" but "I will say what I actually think, including when it's uncomfortable." Not "I am disciplined" but "I do what I said I would do, especially when I don't feel like it." Not "I value loyalty" but "I show up for the people I've committed to, regardless of whether it's convenient."

Keep it short. Five to eight principles, maybe. Anything longer than you can hold in your head is already asking to be forgotten.

Testing the Draft

Once you have a draft, test it against your actual life.

Look at the decisions you've made in the last year — the ones you're proud of and the ones you're not. Does your code explain the proud ones? Does it explain what went wrong in the others? If your code is real, it should be visible in your behavior when you're living well — and its absence should be visible when you're not.

Show the draft to one person you trust to tell you the truth. Not for approval — for pressure. "Here's what I say I stand for. Does this match what you actually observe in me?" That gap, honestly confronted, is some of the most useful information you'll ever get about who you are versus who you think you are.

Revise. The code is not finished. But it is started. And a started code is worth infinitely more than the borrowed values you've been operating on by default.

The Governance Question

Here is the real question your code has to answer: what will you not do?

Not "what do I aspire to" — what will you refuse. The man who knows what he will not do has the most important kind of freedom: the freedom from being available to his worst moments. He has already made the hard decisions in advance, before the pressure arrived. When the moment comes, there is no deliberation.

Write the line. Hold it. That's the whole practice.
